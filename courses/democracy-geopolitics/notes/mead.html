FOUNDATIONS · SESSION 1 · 2014

The Return of Geopolitics

Walter Russell Mead · The Return of Geopolitics: The Revenge of the Revisionist Powers

Central argument

Mead challenges the post-Cold War assumption that traditional hard-power geopolitics had become obsolete. Western leaders interpreted the ideological defeat of communism as evidence that liberal democracy and economic integration had displaced zero-sum power politics.

Russia, China and Iran did not accept the post-Cold War settlement. They continued to pursue strategic interests through military capabilities and realpolitik, challenging the Western-led order and demonstrating the continuing importance of hard power.

Core concepts

Revisionist powers

States that reject the post-Cold War geopolitical settlement and use hard power and realpolitik to alter regional balances of power and challenge US-led frameworks. Mead focuses on Russia, China and Iran.

Liberal world order

The post-Cold War international system championed by the US and its European allies, built around rules-based governance, trade liberalisation, human rights, rule of law, nuclear non-proliferation and international cooperation.

End of History thesis

The idea popularised by Francis Fukuyama that liberal democracy represents the final ideological endpoint of political development, with no competing model capable of sustaining comparable freedom, dignity and long-term dynamism.

Post-historical politics

A foreign-policy outlook that treats territorial boundaries, military deployment and spheres of influence as increasingly obsolete, prioritising cooperation and institutional problem-solving.

Zero-sum geopolitics

Power politics in which one state's strategic, economic or territorial gains are understood as relative losses for rivals, producing competition through military power and coercion.

The early 1990s geopolitical settlement

The Western-led order rested on a particular distribution of power following the Soviet collapse. Mead identifies three regional pillars:

Europe

German reunification, the dissolution of the Soviet Union and the integration of former Warsaw Pact states and Baltic republics into NATO and the EU.

Middle East

Dominance of US-aligned Sunni powers, alongside the strategic double containment of Iran and Iraq.

Asia

US naval and military primacy, supported by bilateral security alliances across the region.

These arrangements reflected the balance of power in the early 1990s. The central miscalculation was to treat that temporary configuration as a permanent condition of international politics.

Western strategic miscalculations

The post-Cold War assumption

Western elites assumed that the defeat of communism had made traditional hard power increasingly irrelevant. They expected non-Western states either to adopt Western liberal principles or to become economically and militarily marginal.

The Clinton administration

Mead identifies a reduction in defence spending, cuts to State Department funding and difficulties securing congressional support for US contributions to the United Nations as expressions of confidence in a self-sustaining global economic and political order.

The George W. Bush administration

Although the attacks of 11 September 2001 forced renewed attention to international conflict, the administration retained post-historical assumptions in its belief that democracy could be rapidly implanted across the Arab Middle East, beginning with the invasion of Iraq.

Revisionist powers and geopolitical competition

Iran

Mead presents Iran as the weakest of the three revisionist powers in conventional economic and military terms, but highly effective in exploiting regional opportunities. The notes emphasise its expansion of influence in Iraq, intervention in Syria alongside Hezbollah and exploitation of divisions among Sunni states.

Russia

Russia is presented as a middling revisionist power that uses its position to disrupt Western projects across the former Soviet Union. The notes identify the war with Georgia in 2008, the annexation of Crimea in 2014, interventions in Ukraine, its role in Armenia and its use of diplomatic and institutional leverage in international politics.

China

The notes identify China as a central revisionist power whose growing military and economic capabilities challenge US regional dominance. Its position also differs from Russia and Iran in important economic and strategic respects.

Why the revisionist powers are not a unified alliance

Despite their shared interest in weakening US dominance, the notes identify four sources of friction:

  • Geopolitical suspicion: Russia's long-term anxieties about China's rise and expanding influence in Central Asia.
  • Ideological divergence: Iran's theocratic worldview differs substantially from China's party-state system and Russia's nationalist autocracy.
  • Economic conflict over energy: Russia and Iran benefit from high oil prices, whereas China, as a major energy importer, has an interest in lower prices.
  • Regional stability: Instability in the Middle East can serve Russian and Iranian interests by distracting Western powers, while creating economic and supply-chain risks for China.

Overall takeaway

Geopolitics did not disappear after the Cold War. The Western assumption that liberal institutions and economic interdependence had displaced power competition underestimated the willingness and capacity of revisionist states to challenge the existing order.

The notes connect this international argument to domestic regime structures: authoritarian consolidation and insulation from external democratising pressure can provide regimes with the security and state capacity to pursue revisionist strategies abroad.
