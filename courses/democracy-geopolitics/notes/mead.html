FOUNDATIONS · SESSION 1 · 2014

The Return of Geopolitics and Revisionist Powers

Walter Russell Mead · Foreign Affairs (2014)

International relations Great power politics Geopolitical revisionism
01

Central argument

Mead challenges the post-Cold War Western assumption that the ideological victory of liberal democracy had rendered traditional hard-power geopolitics obsolete. Western leaders confused the triumph of liberal capitalism over communism with the disappearance of zero-sum competition over territory, military capabilities and regional spheres of influence.

Russia, China and Iran never accepted the geopolitical settlement established after the Soviet collapse. While Western governments increasingly prioritised global governance and institutional cooperation, these revisionist powers continued to use military capabilities and realpolitik to challenge US dominance.

02

Core concepts

Revisionist powers

States that reject the post-Cold War geopolitical settlement and seek to alter regional balances of power through hard power and realpolitik. Mead focuses on Russia, China and Iran.

Liberal world order

The US- and European-led international system built around rules-based governance, trade liberalisation, human rights, the rule of law, nuclear nonproliferation and international cooperation.

End of History thesis

Associated with Francis Fukuyama, the thesis holds that liberal democracy represents the final ideological endpoint of political development, following the defeat of competing ideological models.

Post-historical politics

A Western foreign policy paradigm that treats traditional geopolitical competition, territorial disputes and military power as increasingly obsolete, favouring institutional problem-solving and win-win cooperation.

Zero-sum geopolitics

Classical power politics in which one state's strategic, economic or territorial gains are perceived as losses for its rivals. It is associated with military competition, coercion and struggles over regional influence.

03

The three pillars of the post-Cold War settlement

Mead argues that the liberal world order rested on a set of regional power arrangements established in the aftermath of the Soviet collapse.

Pillar one

Europe

German unification, the dissolution of the Soviet Union and the integration of former Warsaw Pact states and Baltic republics into NATO and the European Union.

Pillar two

Middle East

Dominance of US-aligned Sunni powers, including Saudi Arabia, the Gulf states, Egypt and Turkey, alongside the strategic double containment of Iran and Iraq.

Pillar three

Asia

US naval and military primacy, supported by bilateral security alliances with Japan, South Korea, Australia, Indonesia and other regional partners.

These arrangements reflected the balance of power in the early 1990s. The central Western miscalculation was to treat this temporary configuration as a permanent condition of international politics.

04

Western strategic miscalculations

The assumption that traditional geopolitics had become obsolete shaped successive US administrations, weakening their capacity to defend the international order.

POST-COLD WAR

The original miscalculation

Western elites assumed that the defeat of communism had made hard power obsolete. Non-Western states were expected either to adopt Western liberalism or eventually become economically and militarily irrelevant.

CLINTON

Reduced strategic investment

The administration assumed that the global economic system would largely operate by itself. It pursued substantial defence budget cuts, reduced State Department funding and struggled to secure congressional support for US contributions to the United Nations.

GEORGE W. BUSH

Democracy promotion through intervention

Despite the return of global conflict after 9/11, foreign policy remained influenced by post-historical assumptions. The invasion of Iraq reflected the belief that democracy could be rapidly implanted across the Arab Middle East because history favoured Western values.

OBAMA

Transcending classical geopolitics

Obama prioritised climate treaties, trade agreements, nuclear nonproliferation, arms control with Russia and ending wars in Iraq and Afghanistan. This agenda coincided with defence cuts and reduced engagement in Europe and the Middle East. Attempts to separate Iran's nuclear programme from its regional expansionism were complicated by Saudi and Israeli concerns and Russian intervention in Syria.

05

The internal constraints of status quo powers

European Union

The euro crisis redirected political attention inward, divided public opinion and generated economic strain. The EU's capacity and political will to project strategic influence beyond its borders were impaired.

United States

Foreign policy fatigue after the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan, controversy over surveillance, slow recovery after the 2008 financial crisis and domestic political polarisation weakened support for international leadership.

Revisionist powers

Russia, China and Iran continued to pursue regional influence and challenge US-led arrangements, exploiting the gap between Western ambitions and its capacity to sustain strategic commitments.

06

Revisionist powers and the challenge to US dominance

Russia

Russia challenges the European settlement and US influence in its neighbourhood. Its actions demonstrate that territorial control, military power and regional spheres of influence remain central to international politics.

China

China's growing economic and military capabilities challenge the assumption of uncontested US primacy in Asia. Its rise creates pressure on the regional balance of power and the US alliance system.

Iran

Iran challenges the US-aligned regional order in the Middle East. Its regional ambitions cannot be understood solely through the nuclear question, because its geopolitical influence extends beyond nuclear negotiations.

07

Overall takeaway

THE CENTRAL INSIGHT

The end of the Cold War did not mean the end of geopolitics. The Western-led liberal order was built on a particular balance of power, not on the permanent disappearance of strategic competition. Revisionist powers continued to operate according to hard-power calculations while Western states increasingly acted as if institutional cooperation had replaced them.

Mead's argument is a warning against treating a temporary geopolitical settlement as an irreversible historical outcome. The capacity to sustain international order depends not only on institutions and economic integration, but also on military power, strategic attention and the willingness to defend existing arrangements.
