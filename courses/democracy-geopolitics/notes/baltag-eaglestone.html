Moldova: hedging its bets

How an in-between state navigates great-power rivalry through alternating alignments, strategic manoeuvring, and differentiated integration with the EU and disintegration from Russia.

Dorina Baltag & Amy Eaglestone 2025 European Security, 34(4), 624–645 Read article ↗
Argument in one minute

Moldova is not simply trapped between Russia and the EU. Its geopolitical position creates constraints, but also opportunities for domestic actors to manoeuvre. Baltag and Eaglestone conceptualise this agency through hedging: a repertoire of tactics that changes as EU–Russia relations move from cooperation, to competition, and then conflict. Moldova's trajectory is consequently not a linear westward shift, but a changing pattern of alignment, selective integration, and resistance to Russian influence.

01

The research problem

The article asks how states positioned between competing geopolitical powers navigate changing external pressures. Moldova is a revealing case: its relationship with Russia and the EU has shifted considerably since independence, while its domestic political actors have repeatedly attempted to preserve room for manoeuvre.

Research question

How has Moldova used its geopolitical and geostrategic in-betweenness to navigate the changing dynamics of EU–Russia relations, and what does this mean for its trajectories of integration with the EU and disintegration from Russia?

Empirical approach

The authors draw on 40 semi-structured elite and expert interviews conducted in 2013, 2020, and 2024, alongside documentary evidence, public opinion data, and existing research.

Why Moldova?

Moldova's position between the EU and Russia makes it especially exposed to competing political, economic, and security pressures. Transnistria, energy dependence, and domestic political divisions intensify this condition.

02

Key concepts

Geopolitical in-betweenness

A structural position in which a state is situated between competing geopolitical powers and exposed to their overlapping or conflicting interests. It creates vulnerability and constraint.

Geostrategic in-betweenness

The capacity of domestic actors to leverage that position through selective engagement, institutional adaptation, and coalition-building. It highlights the opportunities for agency within structural constraint.

Hedging

An overarching strategy of avoiding an exclusive commitment to one power. A state combines different forms of cooperation, alignment, and resistance to manage uncertainty and maximise its room for manoeuvre.

Differentiated (dis)integration

Integration and disintegration do not necessarily occur uniformly. States may deepen economic or institutional ties in some areas while delaying, resisting, or reversing alignment in others.

03

The analytical framework

The authors connect structural position to political agency. In-betweenness does not determine a state's behaviour: domestic actors interpret external pressures and deploy different tactics in response.

Geopolitical structure Competing EU and Russian power projections
→
Local agency Political elites, institutions, civil society, and diaspora
→
Hedging tactics Selective alignment, resistance, and manoeuvring

Structural constraint creates the conditions for action; domestic actors determine how those conditions are strategically navigated.

Local agency matters

Political elites, parties, presidents, ministers, and senior officials act as gatekeepers in the adoption of external norms. They can filter, facilitate, or obstruct their implementation. Civil society organisations, diaspora associations, media, and grassroots movements also shape domestic preferences and influence the direction of foreign policy.

04

Hedging tactics in practice

The article's central empirical contribution is its account of how Moldova's hedging tactics change alongside the relationship between the EU and Russia. Rather than treating hedging as one fixed foreign-policy position, the authors identify different tactics across three geopolitical phases.

1990s–early 2000s Cooperation Managing dependence while cautiously exploring EU engagement
2003–2021 Competition Using rivalry to extract benefits and preserve room for manoeuvre
Post-2021 Conflict Moving towards European alignment and actively countering Russia
01

Cooperation: managing vulnerability

1990s–early 2000s · Three tactics

EU–Russia cooperation
Strategic silence Restraint

Avoiding direct confrontation with a more powerful actor on politically sensitive issues.

Moldovan example

Moldova muted demands for the withdrawal of Russian troops stationed in Transnistria, avoiding a direct challenge to Moscow.

Strategic purpose

Preserve sovereignty and room for manoeuvre without provoking Russia.

Manifest emulation Symbolic alignment

Publicly adopting or signalling the practices and orientation of another power without necessarily undertaking deep domestic change.

Moldovan example

Moldova signed the EU Partnership and Cooperation Agreement (PCA) in 1998 and engaged with EU programmes, but without the resources or reforms required for substantial alignment.

Strategic purpose

Signal European orientation while keeping the costs of reform limited.

Bandwagoning Alignment

Aligning with a dominant or influential power to obtain political, economic, or security benefits.

Moldovan example

The Party of Communists' 2001 electoral victory under Vladimir Voronin brought closer relations with Russia. The government considered integration into the Belarus–Russia Union and negotiated directly with Moscow over Transnistria.

Strategic purpose

Secure Russian political and economic support through closer alignment.

What this phase reveals: Moldova's early hedging was cautious. It managed its dependence on Russia while tentatively opening channels towards the EU, without committing fully to either side.
02

Competition: exploiting geopolitical rivalry

2003–2021 · Two tactics

EU–Russia competition
Wedging Playing powers apart

Exploiting divisions between competing powers to gain concessions or prevent one side from consolidating influence.

Moldovan example

Voronin's 2003 refusal to sign Russia's Kozak Memorandum, which proposed federalising Moldova and entrenching Russian leverage, followed domestic and international pressure. Later, extending DCFTA terms to Transnistria offered an EU-backed alternative to Russian influence.

Strategic purpose

Use EU–Russia divisions to resist Russian leverage and obtain concessions.

Multivectorism Multiple alignments

Maintaining relationships with competing powers simultaneously, shifting between them to maximise benefits and avoid exclusive dependence.

Moldovan example

The Alliance for European Integration (AIE) used pro-EU rhetoric while oligarchic networks remained connected to Russian financial interests (2010–15). Igor Dodon also pursued cooperation with Russia while Moldova retained its DCFTA with the EU.

Strategic purpose

Balance competing relationships and maximise short-term benefits while limiting risks.

What this phase reveals: As EU–Russia rivalry intensified, Moldova's tactics became more assertive and adaptive. Domestic elites attempted to turn geopolitical competition into political and economic advantages, although these manoeuvres did not necessarily produce a coherent long-term strategy.
03

Conflict: contesting Russian influence

Post-2021 · Two tactics

EU–Russia conflict
Contestation Active resistance

Actively challenging the influence and power projection of a competing actor, rather than accommodating it.

Moldovan example

Moldova aligned with EU sanctions regimes, rejected Dodon's pro-Russian political rhetoric, and reoriented diplomatic and strategic relationships towards Ukraine and Romania.

Strategic purpose

Signal a more assertive break with Russian influence and strengthen European alignment.

Hard balancing Reducing dependence

Building capabilities and partnerships to reduce the leverage that a threatening power can exercise.

Moldovan example

Following the 2022 energy crisis, Moldova diversified energy supplies, including imports from Romania. It also received security-related support through the EU Partnership Mission (EUPM) and European Peace Facility (EPF).

Strategic purpose

Reduce Russian leverage in critical energy and security sectors.

What this phase reveals: The relationship has shifted from managing competing ties to actively countering Russian power projection. Moldova's hedging repertoire now includes more direct alignment with the EU and measures to reduce strategic dependence on Russia.
The evolution of hedging

Restraint → Manoeuvring → Resistance. Moldova's tactics evolve with the geopolitical environment: initially accommodating both powers, then exploiting their competition, and finally contesting Russian influence while deepening its European alignment. Hedging is therefore not synonymous with neutrality or simple oscillation between East and West. It is a changing repertoire of strategies through which domestic actors seek to preserve agency under external pressure.

05

The European pivot

Since 2021, Moldova's foreign-policy orientation has shifted significantly. The PAS government under Maia Sandu has pursued a more explicit European trajectory, accelerated by Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

2021

PAS comes to power

The electoral victory of the Party of Action and Solidarity creates the political basis for a more decisive pro-European direction.

2022

War and energy vulnerability

Russia's invasion of Ukraine intensifies Moldova's security concerns and exposes the risks of dependence on Russian energy. Diversification becomes a central strategic priority.

2023

European Political Community summit

Hosting the European Political Community summit in June 2023 demonstrates Moldova's growing diplomatic visibility and closer engagement with European partners.

2024

European integration becomes a domestic political battleground

The EU referendum and presidential election reveal the importance of domestic polarisation, Russian interference, and diaspora mobilisation in determining Moldova's European trajectory.

The diaspora as a counterweight

The diaspora has become an important transnational actor in Moldova's political trajectory. Remittances amounted to USD 1.592 billion in 2022, while diaspora mobilisation has influenced electoral outcomes and supported reform. In 2024, more than 82% of diaspora voters supported Maia Sandu and the EU referendum, counterbalancing domestic opposition to European integration.

06

Differentiated integration and disintegration

Moldova's changing foreign-policy orientation also produces uneven patterns of integration. The country can deepen economic and technological cooperation with the EU without immediately achieving equivalent political or security integration.

Integration with the EU

Moldova initially prioritised economic and technological cooperation through the Association Agreement and DCFTA, while delaying stronger political alignment. More recently, the process has become increasingly political, particularly following Russia's invasion of Ukraine and Moldova's pursuit of EU accession.

Disintegration from Russia

Moldova has moved away from Russian-led political and economic projects, while reducing energy dependence and aligning more closely with European partners. This de-alignment has been accompanied by Russian interference and domestic contestation.

The risk of differentiated disintegration

A future pro-Russian government could seek to retain the economic benefits of the EU single market while rejecting deeper political integration and EU standards. The authors warn that Moldova could therefore relapse into multivectorism: maintaining economic integration with the EU alongside renewed political alignment with Russia.

Integration is inherently political

The Moldovan case challenges the assumption that differentiated integration is simply a technical process involving different speeds of convergence. Domestic politics, geopolitical pressures, institutional capacity, and strategic priorities all shape the form and depth of integration.

07

Theoretical implications

1. In-betweenness is both constraint and opportunity

Moldova's geopolitical position exposes it to external pressure, but it also creates opportunities for domestic actors to exploit competition between great powers. The state is not merely acted upon: it can exercise agency within structural constraints.

2. Hedging is more than balancing or bandwagoning

The article expands the understanding of hedging by identifying a repertoire of tactics that changes across geopolitical contexts. Strategic silence, manifest emulation, bandwagoning, wedging, multivectorism, contestation, and hard balancing capture different ways of navigating in-betweenness.

3. Domestic actors shape geopolitical alignment

Political elites, institutions, civil society, and transnational actors influence how external pressures are interpreted and translated into policy. Alignment is therefore not simply imposed from outside; it is mediated through domestic political processes.

4. Hedging requires a long-term strategic vision

Although hedging can produce short-term benefits, reactive manoeuvring can undermine consistency. The authors emphasise the importance of domestic reforms, tangible benefits for citizens, and the contribution of transnational actors such as the diaspora.

08

Overall takeaway

Moldova is an agent within geopolitical in-betweenness, not merely its victim.

Baltag and Eaglestone show how Moldova's strategies have evolved in response to changing EU–Russia relations. Its early caution gave way to more active manoeuvring during the period of competition, followed by a decisive European pivot under conditions of conflict. Yet this trajectory remains politically contingent. Domestic polarisation, economic vulnerabilities, Russian interference, and unresolved territorial questions continue to constrain Moldova's choices. The central insight is that geopolitical position matters, but domestic agency determines how that position is navigated.
