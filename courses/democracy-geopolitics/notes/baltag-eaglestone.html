DEMOCRACY & GEOPOLITICS / MOLDOVA

Furthest away from Russia and ever closer to the EU? Moldova hedging its bets on alternating alignments and (differentiated) dis/integration

Geopolitical in-betweenness, hedging and Moldova's changing external alignments

Dorina Baltag & Amy Eaglestone (2025) · European Security, 34(4), 624–645 · Publication ↗

CENTRAL ARGUMENT

Moldova's position between Russia and the EU is not simply a condition of geopolitical vulnerability. It also creates room for strategic manoeuvre. The authors argue that Moldovan elites have used different forms of hedging as EU–Russia relations shifted from cooperation, to competition, and then conflict. Since 2021, Moldova has moved towards a more decisive European alignment, although domestic divisions and its unresolved geopolitical vulnerabilities continue to complicate this trajectory.

Core concepts

STRUCTURAL POSITION

Geopolitical in-betweenness

A state's position between competing geopolitical powers, creating external constraints and pressures on its political and strategic choices.

STATE AGENCY

Geostrategic in-betweenness

The capacity of an in-between state to use its position strategically through adaptation, selective engagement and manoeuvring.

FOREIGN POLICY

Hedging

A strategy that avoids committing fully to one competing power, combining different forms of cooperation, ambiguity and resistance.

STRATEGIC PRACTICE

Hedging tactics

Specific foreign-policy actions through which states manage uncertainty and preserve room for manoeuvre between competing powers.

EU RELATIONS

Differentiated integration

Uneven participation in EU structures and policies, with economic, political and institutional integration progressing at different speeds.

EXTERNAL ALIGNMENT

Dis-integration

The gradual distancing from, or withdrawal of, political, economic and institutional ties with an external power.

1. From geopolitical constraint to strategic agency

The authors connect two dimensions of Moldova's in-between position: the structural pressures created by great-power rivalry and the agency exercised by domestic political actors.

Geopolitical position Competing EU and Russian influence
→
Hedging Strategic choices and tactical manoeuvres
→
Alignment trajectories Integration with the EU and dis-integration from Russia

The relationship between structure, agency and differentiated (dis)integration

Geopolitical in-betweenness does not make Moldova a passive object of external competition. Its elites have sought to exploit the opportunities created by rivalry, adjusting their strategies as the relationship between Moscow and Brussels changes. Hedging captures this active response to structural constraints.

2. Three phases of EU–Russia relations

The article organises Moldova's foreign-policy trajectory around three changing configurations of EU–Russia relations.

1990s – EARLY 2000s

Cooperation

Moldova navigated an environment in which the EU and Russia could both project influence without an openly exclusive geopolitical contest.

Strategic silence · Manifest emulation · Bandwagoning

2003–2021

Competition

As both powers increasingly sought influence in the region, Moldova used its position to manoeuvre between them and extract political and economic benefits.

Wedging · Multivectorism

POST-2021

Conflict

Russia's war against Ukraine intensified the geopolitical confrontation. Moldova moved towards European alignment and more direct resistance to Russian influence.

Contestation · Hard balancing

3. Moldova's hedging tactics

The tactics change across the three phases. They show how Moldova's elites used ambiguity, selective cooperation and resistance to manage external pressure.

COOPERATION · 1990s–EARLY 2000s

Strategic silence

Avoiding direct confrontation with Russia, including muted demands for the withdrawal of Russian troops from Transnistria.

COOPERATION · 1990s–EARLY 2000s

Manifest emulation

Symbolic engagement with European structures, including the 1998 Partnership and Cooperation Agreement, without committing to deep reforms.

COOPERATION · 1990s–EARLY 2000s

Bandwagoning

The Communist Party's alignment with Russia after 2001, including consideration of closer association with the Belarus–Russia Union.

COMPETITION · 2003–2021

Wedging

Using divisions between the EU and Russia to gain room for manoeuvre. The rejection of the Kozak Memorandum in 2003 is a key example.

COMPETITION · 2003–2021

Multivectorism

Maintaining relationships with both powers. The AIE coalition combined public EU-oriented rhetoric with continuing reliance on Russian financial networks; Igor Dodon also cooperated with Russia while retaining the DCFTA framework.

CONFLICT · POST-2021

Contestation

Actively rejecting Russian influence, aligning with EU sanctions and reorienting relations towards Ukraine and Romania under Maia Sandu.

CONFLICT · POST-2021

Hard balancing

Reducing Russian leverage through energy diversification, including imports from Romania, and greater European security cooperation.

4. European integration and Russian dis-integration

Moldova's changing external alignment is not a simple, simultaneous shift across every policy area. Economic, political and security relations have developed unevenly.

Closer integration with the EU

The Association Agreement and DCFTA initially enabled economic and technological cooperation without requiring equally rapid political and security integration. The 2022 membership application, candidate status and opening of accession negotiations marked a more decisive European trajectory.

Dis-integration from Russia

Moldova has increasingly distanced itself from Russian-led political and economic projects. The full-scale invasion of Ukraine accelerated this process, alongside energy diversification and a reduction in official ties with Moscow.

The authors stress that differentiated integration is politically contingent. Moldova's capacity gaps, domestic polarisation and geopolitical pressures shape the pace and depth of integration. Its trajectory therefore cannot be understood as a purely technical process of adopting EU rules.

5. Domestic constraints and the limits of the European pivot

Although Moldova's post-2021 orientation represents a substantial shift, the authors do not treat European alignment as an irreversible or uncontested outcome.

Russian interference, societal polarisation and economic vulnerabilities continue to challenge the consolidation of public support. Transnistria and Gagauzia complicate the country's geopolitical position, while cautious domestic rhetoric around defence integration reflects the sensitivity of closer Western alignment.

The authors also identify the diaspora as an important transnational actor. Its financial, political and social engagement can reinforce Moldova's European aspirations and support the country's reform trajectory.

Methodology

The article uses a qualitative case-study design, drawing on 40 semi-structured elite and expert interviews conducted in three waves: 2013, 2020 and 2024. Interview material is triangulated with documentary evidence, including government and non-government reports, opinion polls, policy documents and media sources.

Overall takeaway

Moldova's in-between position has been both a source of vulnerability and an opportunity for strategic agency. Its hedging tactics have changed with the evolution of EU–Russia relations: from pragmatic ambiguity during cooperation, to manoeuvring during competition, and increasingly assertive European alignment during conflict. The central question is whether this pivot can be consolidated domestically, rather than whether Moldova can simply choose between East and West.
