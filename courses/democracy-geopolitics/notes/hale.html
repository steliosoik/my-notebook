HISTORICAL LEGACIES · SESSION 3 · 2015

Patronal Politics and the Power of Expectations

Henry E. Hale · The central role of expectations in sustaining political power

Patronalism Informal institutions Political networks Collective action
01

Central argument

Hale argues that political power in patronalistic societies is sustained through personal networks and, crucially, people's expectations about whether others will remain loyal. Formal institutions, ideology and material resources matter, but they cannot independently explain how political power is maintained or lost.

The central question is not simply what resources a leader controls. It is whether the people within their network believe that others will continue to obey them. Political power is therefore a social equilibrium, reproduced through mutually reinforcing expectations.

02

Core concepts

Patronalism

A social equilibrium in which individuals organise political and economic activities primarily through personal relationships and exchanges of concrete rewards and punishments.

Patron-client relationship

An unequal relationship in which a patron provides resources, protection or opportunities in exchange for a client's loyalty and support.

Patronal network

A network of personal relationships connecting patrons, clients and other individuals. These networks are often hierarchical but can extend across different political and economic institutions.

Social equilibrium

A stable pattern of behaviour that persists because individuals expect others to behave in the same way.

Self-fulfilling prophecy

An expectation that helps produce the outcome it predicts. People support a leader because they expect others to support them, thereby making that leader stronger.

Coordination dilemma

A situation in which an individual's best decision depends on what others decide to do, making collective expectations essential.

Network investment

The time, resources, trust and personal obligations accumulated through participation in a network. These investments make leaving an established network costly.

Patronalism is not simply corruption

Hale distinguishes patronalism from corruption, clientelism and weak rule of law. These phenomena may accompany patronalism, but they are narrower concepts. Patronalism describes the underlying social equilibrium organised around personalised relationships.

For example, favouring relatives or using personal connections may be legal in one context and illegal in another. What matters analytically is the personalised nature of the relationship, not necessarily its legal status.

04

The power of expectations

Existing explanations of patronal networks often emphasise the patron's resources, capacity to monitor clients and ability to punish disloyalty. Hale argues that these factors are insufficient.

A patron may possess enormous resources and organisational power but still lose control if clients stop believing that others will obey. Expectations determine whether resources can actually be mobilised to exercise power.

05

The collapse of communist regimes in 1989

Hale draws on Mancur Olson's explanation of the collapse of communist regimes in Eastern Europe. These regimes possessed extensive control over resources, surveillance and coercion. If resources and organisational capacity alone determined political stability, they should have survived.

Instead, changing expectations among officials and clients undermined the willingness to defend the regimes. Hale describes this dynamic as a kind of bank run on the state.

1
Changing political signals

Gorbachev's reforms and his refusal to intervene to preserve other socialist regimes changed how officials assessed the regime's future.

2
Declining expectations of survival

Political elites, police officers and other officials increasingly believed their governments might collapse.

3
Withdrawal of loyalty

Officials became less willing to obey orders or punish opposition, weakening the regime's capacity to maintain control.

4
Self-reinforcing collapse

As more people expected the regime to fall, fewer were willing to defend it. The expectation of collapse helped produce the collapse itself.

06

Why networks are difficult to abandon

If expectations determine which networks are powerful, individuals should theoretically switch networks whenever another becomes more promising. Hale explains why this does not happen easily: networks are relatively sticky.

Individuals accumulate trust, reputation, influence, favours and personal obligations through long-term participation. Leaving means potentially losing these investments and entering another network without the same established relationships.

Why people remain

Established trust, reputation, reciprocal obligations, influence and accumulated personal relationships make existing networks valuable.

Why people defect

When expectations about the future of a network change, the value of remaining can decline and individuals may begin to leave.

Hale does not treat ethnicity or kinship as inherently decisive. They matter because they can represent substantial investments in existing relationships. Even these networks can experience defections when expectations change.

07

Formal institutions and informal networks

Patronal networks frequently extend beyond the boundaries of individual institutions. A person may hold little formal authority but exercise considerable political influence through personal connections.

Elections, constitutions and government structures therefore do not necessarily determine how power operates in practice. Their effects depend partly on the expectations and relationships of the actors operating within them.

08

Methodology and theoretical contribution

The chapter develops a theoretical, ideal-type framework drawing on collective action theory, coordination dilemmas and self-fulfilling expectations.

It uses historical illustrations, particularly the collapse of communist regimes in 1989, to demonstrate how expectations can coordinate loyalty and undermine political authority.

09

Overall takeaway

THE CENTRAL INSIGHT

Political power depends not simply on what a leader controls, but on whether others believe that the leader will continue to control it. Expectations coordinate loyalty, and coordinated loyalty produces power.
