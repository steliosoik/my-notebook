DEMOCRACY & GEOPOLITICS / SESSION 4

Mapping Plurality in Moldova

Ethnic and linguistic diversity beyond the narrative of societal division

Eleanor Knott (2025)

CENTRAL ARGUMENT

Ethnic and linguistic plurality does not necessarily produce societal division. Knott challenges representations of Moldova as a deeply divided society by distinguishing political narratives about identity from the more complex realities of everyday social interaction. Census data and a bottom-up perspective reveal how distinct identities can coexist with bilingualism, cooperation and shared social practices.

Core concepts

DIVERSITY

Plurality

The coexistence of different identities, languages and social affiliations within a society.

SOCIAL RELATIONS

Societal division

A condition in which differences become meaningful boundaries separating groups and shaping their relationships.

NATIONAL IDENTITY

Titular ethnicity / language

The ethnicity or language associated with the dominant national group and national identity.

TOP-DOWN

Top-down approach

Understanding identity and division through political institutions, elites and national narratives.

BOTTOM-UP

Bottom-up approach

Examining how ordinary people experience, negotiate and manage differences in everyday life.

1. Questioning the divided-society narrative

Moldova is frequently characterised through ethnic and linguistic divisions, particularly those involving Moldovan, Romanian and Russian identities. Knott questions whether political disputes over identity necessarily reflect how ordinary citizens experience social relations.

THE POLITICAL REPRESENTATION

Top-down perspective

Political institutions and elites construct narratives about national identity, language and belonging.

  • Political disputes over national identity
  • Contestation over language and symbols
  • Identity boundaries emphasised in national narratives

EVERYDAY SOCIAL REALITY

Bottom-up perspective

Ordinary people navigate multiple identities and languages through everyday interaction.

  • Bilingualism and multilingual communication
  • Shared social practices
  • Cooperation across identity boundaries

Historical context: Soviet nationality policies institutionalised distinctions between Moldovan and Romanian identity. These legacies help explain why national identity and language remain politically contested. However, political contestation should not automatically be treated as evidence of everyday societal division.

2. What the 2014 census reveals

Knott uses the 2014 census to examine ethnicity, native language and language use across Moldova's regions. The crucial analytical point is that these are related, but distinct, dimensions of identity.

DIMENSION 01

Ethnicity

How individuals identify ethnically. Most regions have a Moldovan ethnic majority, although important regional differences exist.

DIMENSION 02

Native language

The language an individual identifies as their native language. This does not necessarily determine their everyday language use.

DIMENSION 03

Language use

The language individuals actually use in communication. It may extend beyond their ethnic or native-language identification.

Key distinction: Identifying a language as one's native language does not necessarily determine which language one uses in everyday communication. Language use is therefore not a straightforward indicator of ethnic identification.

3. Regional patterns of plurality

The census reveals regional variation rather than a single, uniform pattern of ethnic and linguistic composition.

Most regions

Most regions have a Moldovan ethnic majority, although the relationship between ethnicity and language use varies.

Gagauzia

Notable for its Gagauz population, illustrating the regional diversity within Moldova.

Taraclia

Notable for its Bulgarian population, providing another example of regional plurality.

Urban areas

Generally exhibit greater linguistic diversity, with Russian and Romanian more prominent than in rural areas.

Russian as a common language: Russian is widely used beyond the ethnic Russian population, suggesting that it functions as a common language of inter-ethnic communication.

4. From identity boundaries to everyday interaction

The article shifts attention from the existence of different identities to the ways people interact across them.

Distinct identities

People maintain different ethnic and linguistic affiliations.

→

Everyday multilingualism

Multiple languages facilitate communication across identity boundaries.

→

Social cooperation

Shared practices and interaction can coexist with distinct identities.

People can maintain distinct ethnic or linguistic identities without treating those differences as obstacles to social interaction. Plurality should therefore not be confused with conflict.

Methodological contribution

Knott combines census data from 2014 with a bottom-up perspective on everyday social relations. By distinguishing ethnicity, native language and language use across regions, the analysis moves beyond political representations of identity and examines how plurality is experienced in practice.

Overall takeaway

Knott challenges the assumption that ethnic and linguistic diversity necessarily produces societal division. The key analytical question is not simply whether different identities exist, but whether and how those differences become meaningful boundaries in social life. A society can contain substantial identity diversity while remaining characterised by bilingualism, cooperation and shared everyday practices.
