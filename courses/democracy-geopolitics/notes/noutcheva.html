Geopolitical contestation · Reading notes

Whose Legitimacy?

Gergana Noutcheva (2018) · “The EU and Russia in Contest for the Eastern Neighbourhood”

The EU and Russia influence political developments in Eastern Europe not only through economic incentives, sanctions or coercion, but also through competing political norms and understandings of legitimate authority. Both actors shape domestic political competition by legitimising opposing models of governance. Ukraine illustrates how these competing external influences can leave a lasting imprint on a country's democratic trajectory.

Core concepts

External influence

External soft influence

The capacity of external actors to shape domestic developments non-coercively by promoting political models, norms and ideas considered legitimate or worthy of emulation.

Acceptance

Political legitimacy

Societal acceptance of particular forms of political authority and governance as appropriate. It matters for democratic consolidation and authoritarian survival.

Learning

Socialisation

The gradual adoption of external norms, beliefs and understandings of appropriate political behaviour through communication, interaction and learning.

Analytical distinction

Actorness vs. structure

Actorness concerns deliberate actions by external and domestic actors; structure concerns the broader influence of political norms, values and institutional environments. Both must be examined together.

Norms

Normative power

The ability to influence others through the attractiveness and perceived legitimacy of political values and governance models, rather than material power alone.

Four mechanisms of external soft influence

Noutcheva organises four complementary mechanisms around the interaction between two dimensions: external agency and norms, and domestic agency and norms (pp. 315–316).

The framework identifies which combination drives each mechanism.

External agency + domestic agency
1 · Persuasion

Persuasion of domestic actors

External actors deliberately engage domestic political, economic and societal elites to transfer ideas, policy frameworks and governance practices through communication, deliberation and social learning.

Examples: EU Eastern Partnership, Association Agreements and institutional dialogues; Russia's Eurasian Economic Union, cultural organisations and religious networks (pp. 316–317).

2 · Legitimisation

Legitimisation of domestic policies and practices

External powers use diplomatic statements and political communication to shape societal perceptions of legitimate governance.

Contrast: The EU endorses democratic improvements and condemns malpractice. Russia portrays Western democracy promotion and externally supported transitions as illegitimate interference, particularly in its criticism of the Colour Revolutions (p. 318).

3 · Appropriation

Legitimisation of domestic actions

Domestic actors invoke external political models to justify their own policies and gain public support.

Contrast: Pro-European politicians invoke EU democratic standards; pro-Russian actors draw on state paternalism, traditional values and centralised authority. The focus is domestic appropriation, not simply external promotion (pp. 318–319).

4 · Diffusion

Diffusion of ideas

Political ideas spread across borders through imitation, emulation and exposure to external examples, without requiring deliberate intervention by external actors.

Examples: Social media, satellite television and digital communication transmit both democratic protest movements and authoritarian governance practices (p. 319).

How the mechanisms interact

The four mechanisms are complementary rather than mutually exclusive. Influence can involve deliberate external engagement, domestic political appropriation and the wider circulation of norms at the same time.

The result depends on domestic receptiveness and on whether competing political models are perceived as legitimate.

Ukraine: empirical application

Ukraine demonstrates how EU and Russian influence operate simultaneously through formal institutions, informal networks, domestic political actors and the diffusion of ideas.

European Union

Institutional engagement and democratic legitimacy

Following the Orange Revolution (2004–05), the EU expanded engagement through the European Neighbourhood Policy, Association Agenda and Eastern Partnership. During Euromaidan (2013–14), diplomatic support provided symbolic legitimacy to protesters demanding accountable governance (pp. 320–322).

Russia

Informal networks and alternative legitimacy

Russia relied on political, economic and cultural networks, including energy-sector connections, pro-Kremlin organisations, Russian-language media and the Orthodox Church. Its narrative presented Euromaidan as an illegitimate, Western-backed coup (pp. 321–322).

Domestic appropriation and electoral competition

Ukrainian political parties incorporated European or Russian political identities into their electoral strategies. The 2004 and 2010 elections reflected competing visions of European liberal democracy and Russian-associated state paternalism. Following Euromaidan, the 2014 election demonstrated a significant shift towards pro-European political forces (pp. 323–324).

Diffusion across borders

Euromaidan borrowed organisational practices from international protest movements. Facebook supported domestic coordination, while Twitter helped attract international attention. Conversely, Russian restrictions on NGOs and protests inspired authoritarian legislation under Yanukovych in 2014 (p. 324).

Ukraine's changing political trajectory

The EU expands institutional engagement with Ukraine following the revolution.

2010 · Competing political models

Electoral competition reflects opposing European and Russian-associated visions of governance.

2013–14 · Euromaidan

EU diplomatic support lends symbolic legitimacy to protesters; Russia contests the legitimacy of the movement.

2014 · Political realignment

The election demonstrates a significant shift towards pro-European political forces.

After Crimea and Donbas

Russia's appeal declines dramatically following its actions in Crimea and Donbas.

Legitimacy and democratic trajectory

Neither external actor can independently determine Ukraine's political trajectory. Their influence depends on domestic receptiveness and the legitimacy of their political models. Noutcheva concludes that competition between opposing external influences reinforced domestic political competition and may ultimately have improved Ukraine's long-term prospects for democratisation (pp. 325–326).

Core takeaway

Democratisation cannot be understood exclusively through material incentives or domestic political institutions. The legitimacy of competing political models, their domestic appropriation and their diffusion across societies are equally important. External influence is not simply imposed from outside: it is mediated, interpreted and contested by domestic actors.
