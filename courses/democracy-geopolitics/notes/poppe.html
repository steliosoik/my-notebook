Beyond Contestation

Conceptualizing Negotiation in Democracy Promotion

Annika Elena Poppe, Julia Leininger & Jonas Wolff · 2019 · Democratization, 26(5), pp. 777–795
Central argument

Democracy promotion should be understood as an interactive and ongoing process of negotiation between external promoters and local actors, rather than the unilateral transfer of democratic norms and institutions. Recipient actors possess agency to contest, reinterpret and reshape externally promoted policies.

Core concepts

Democracy promotionExternal efforts aimed at supporting liberalisation of authoritarian regimes and the establishment, deepening or stabilisation of democratic regimes.
Democracy promotion negotiationCommunicative interaction between external democracy promoters and local actors involving the exchange of demands, arguments and proposals in pursuit of agreement.
Local agencyThe capacity of recipient-country actors to actively shape, challenge, reinterpret or reject externally promoted democratic policies.
Norm contestationThe process through which actors challenge the meaning, legitimacy or applicability of established international norms.
Norm localisationThe adaptation and reinterpretation of international norms to fit local political, cultural and institutional contexts.
Official and unofficial negotiationsOfficial negotiations occur in formal institutional settings and seek explicit agreements. Unofficial exchanges are less formal and may produce tacit understandings.
Negotiation outputThe immediate results of negotiations, particularly whether an agreement is reached, its form, scope and substance.
Negotiation outcomeThe subsequent consequences of agreements or disagreements for democracy promotion practices and the recipient country's political regime.
The authors combine International Relations scholarship on norm contestation, appropriation and localisation with democratisation research that challenges linear understandings of political transitions. Negotiation relies on communication and involves at least some implicit exchange, except under conditions of pure coercion or passive acceptance.

Fundamental norms and principles

What shapes negotiations?

The framework distinguishes characteristics of the actors involved from the wider context in which they interact.

Recipient regime type

Expected to have a curvilinear relationship with negotiation depth. Highly authoritarian regimes may view meaningful negotiation as threatening to regime survival, while highly democratic regimes may have little substantive disagreement with promoters. Intermediate regimes offer greater potential for substantive negotiations.

Domestic strength of recipient governments

Also expected to have a curvilinear relationship. Governments that are neither overwhelmingly strong nor too weak are more likely to negotiate meaningfully: they can resist external demands while remaining susceptible to external pressure.

Promoter commitment

The importance and institutional centralisation of democracy promotion in the external actor's foreign policy increase the likelihood of substantive negotiations.

Two curvilinear relationships

For recipient regime type and domestic government strength, the authors expect the potential for deeper negotiations to be greatest at intermediate levels, rather than at either extreme.

Contextual conditions

Power asymmetries

External actors possess material and political leverage, but negotiations can empower weaker recipients because reaching an agreement requires their participation. The authors expect greater asymmetries to increase the likelihood of in-depth negotiations.

Normative setting

Existing international commitments to sovereignty and non-interference strengthen recipients. Established human rights and democracy obligations strengthen promoters. Strong, previously agreed democratic norms reduce the need for deeper negotiations.

Normative convergence and divergence

Greater differences in cultural understandings, political values and conceptions of democracy increase the likelihood that fundamental normative issues become contested.

Historical relationship

The history of interaction between the parties, including colonial legacies and previous relationships, shapes the negotiation context.

Regional and global environment

The wider international context matters, including the influence of alternative powers such as China and Russia.

Negotiation results: output versus outcome

An agreement is not the same thing as its consequences. The authors distinguish the immediate results of negotiations from what subsequently happens to democracy promotion and the recipient regime.

Negotiation output

Was an agreement reached? Was it official or informal, explicit or tacit, full or partial? Whose demands shaped its substance?

→

Negotiation outcome

What consequences did the agreement or disagreement have for democracy promotion policies and practices, and for the recipient country's political regime?

An unresolved question: Do deeper negotiations and greater local ownership improve democratic effectiveness, or can they instead produce compromises that dilute democracy promotion?

Negotiation is not a linear process with a definitive endpoint. It is continuous and overlaps with the implementation and adjustment of democracy promotion itself.

Core takeaway

Democracy promotion is not simply something external actors do to recipient countries. It emerges through ongoing negotiation between actors with different interests, power resources and understandings of democracy. Understanding its effectiveness therefore requires examining what is negotiated, what shapes the process and what consequences negotiations produce.
