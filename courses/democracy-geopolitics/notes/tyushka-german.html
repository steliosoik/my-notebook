Geostrategic “Inbetweenness”

As a (Mis)fortune of Eastern European Neighbours of Russia and the European Union

Andriy Tyushka & Tracey German · 2025 · pp. 520–531
Central argument

Eastern European states situated between the EU and Russia are not merely geographical buffer zones or passive objects of great-power competition. Their position creates structural vulnerabilities, political polarisation and difficult foreign-policy choices, but it can also provide opportunities to exercise agency and influence regional power dynamics. The authors distinguish between geopolitical inbetweenness as a structural condition and geostrategic inbetweenness as the ways states respond to it.

Core concepts

Geopolitical inbetweennessA state's position between competing great powers, understood not simply geographically but also through political identity, international status and strategic environment. It is socially and politically constructed through great-power interaction.
Geostrategic inbetweennessThe strategic responses through which states navigate, exploit or attempt to escape their geopolitical position. It transforms inbetweenness from a structural constraint into a potential instrument of statecraft.
LiminalityAn ambiguous and unsettled condition of belonging, in which a state may identify with one power, remain between competing powers or partially belong to both.
Ontological securityA state's need for continuity, identity and a relatively stable understanding of its place in the international system. Inbetweenness threatens this stability through persistent uncertainty.
Shelter diplomacySeeking protection or security guarantees from external powers, formally through alliances or informally through strategic partnerships.
ObjectificationThe reduction of in-between states to instruments of great-power competition, denying their agency and potentially undermining their sovereignty.

From geopolitical condition to strategic agency

The central distinction is between where a state is situated in the international order and what it does with that position.

Structural condition

Geopolitical inbetweenness

States occupy a contested environment in which EU and Russian interests overlap and increasingly conflict. Their location, identity and political orientation are shaped by competing integration projects and external expectations.

Strategic response

Geostrategic inbetweenness

States navigate, exploit or attempt to escape their position. They can adjust alignment, seek external shelter, contest great-power expectations or use their intermediary position to preserve room for manoeuvre.

Key analytical shift: Inbetweenness is not only something that happens to states. It can also become something states strategically act upon.

More than geography: competing geopolitical configurations

The authors focus on the EU's six Eastern Partnership neighbours. Their positions cannot be understood through physical location alone: identity, political orientation and competing integration projects also matter. These identities are fluid rather than permanent.

Armenia

One of the six Eastern Partnership neighbours navigating the contested space between Russian and European influence.

Azerbaijan

A state whose strategic position provides opportunities for pragmatic equidistance and partnerships beyond the EU and Russia.

Belarus

A neighbour whose multi-vector manoeuvring became increasingly difficult as EU–Russia relations deteriorated.

Georgia

A state whose foreign-policy orientation and external relationships illustrate the challenges of navigating competing geopolitical expectations.

Moldova

A state balancing external pressures while pursuing EU accession and differentiated integration.

Ukraine

A state confronting the existential implications of competing geopolitical projects and the defence of sovereignty.

Strategic significance and objectification

In-between states matter because of their strategic importance to competing powers, not necessarily because of their size or military capabilities. They become arenas for competing visions of regional order.

Buffer zones

States situated between competing powers, theoretically reducing tensions through neutrality. Historically, however, they have had mixed success in maintaining stability.

Grey zones

Contested spaces where different identities, loyalties and political orientations coexist.

Spheres of influence

Exclusive areas of political, military and economic control claimed by a great power. These directly threaten the sovereignty of states such as Ukraine.

The consequences of inbetweenness

Pressure to choose

Choices can become existential questions of national identity and survival. Choosing often means losing.

Domestic polarisation

Domestic political divisions frequently mirror geopolitical divisions, particularly between pro-European and pro-Russian orientations.

Distinctive security cultures

States develop particular understandings of security and strategic vulnerability through their position between competing powers.

Shelter diplomacy

States seek third-party partnerships and security guarantees to reduce vulnerability. Examples include Azerbaijan's relationship with Türkiye and Belarus's and Georgia's outreach to China.

Strategic flexibility

Foreign-policy responses can shift as states attempt to preserve sovereignty and room for manoeuvre.

Opportunities for agency

Great-power rivalry can paradoxically create opportunities for smaller states to influence the competition itself, rather than merely being shaped by it.

Three strategic postures

The authors identify three ways in which in-between states can respond to their geopolitical environment. These postures are not necessarily mutually exclusive or permanent.

Posture 01

Consent

Accepting or accommodating the existing geopolitical configuration.

Posture 02

Contestation

Challenging the expectations or influence of competing powers.

Posture 03

Counteraction

Actively resisting external pressures and attempting to alter the strategic situation.

Theoretical framework: realist constructivism

Material constraints and socially constructed meaning

The authors employ realist constructivism, combining the material power and structural constraints emphasised by realism with the importance of identity, norms and socially constructed understandings emphasised by constructivism.

Konstruktpolitik describes the attempt to change the social and ideational environment in order to compensate for material limitations. Geography cannot easily be changed, but the political meaning of geography can. States can reshape how external powers perceive their identity, sovereignty and strategic role.

Comparative country trajectories

Belarus

Lukashenka's multi-vector manoeuvring became increasingly untenable as EU–Russia relations deteriorated, pushing Belarus towards Russia.

Armenia and Georgia

Their different responses to changing geopolitical circumstances demonstrate that similar structural pressures can produce different strategic choices.

Moldova

Uses balancing, hedging and differentiated integration to navigate competing external pressures while pursuing EU accession.

Ukraine

Illustrates the dangers of unsuccessful hedging and underbalancing, ultimately facing existential war to defend sovereignty.

Azerbaijan

Exploits its position through pragmatic equidistance between the EU and Russia, strategic alignment with Türkiye and energy-connectivity projects.

Core takeaway

Inbetweenness is simultaneously a constraint and an opportunity. The foreign-policy behaviour of these states should not automatically be interpreted as inconsistency or indecision. Strategic shifts, hedging and U-turns can constitute rational attempts to preserve sovereignty, maintain room for manoeuvre and survive great-power competition.
