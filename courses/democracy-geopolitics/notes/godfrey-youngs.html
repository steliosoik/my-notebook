Geopolitical contestation · Democracy support

Strengthening Democracy Support in Regimes With Dominant Parties

Godfrey & Youngs (2021) · Carnegie Endowment for International Peace

Godfrey and Youngs examine the growing challenge dominant-party regimes pose to international democracy promotion. Drawing on Georgia, Mozambique, Nicaragua and Zimbabwe, they argue that conventional democracy assistance can inadvertently strengthen incumbent parties when those parties capture state institutions and external resources. Democracy support must therefore move beyond institutional capacity-building and focus more directly on political competition, opposition parties, elections and civic activism.

Core concepts

Regime structure

Dominant-party regime

A political system in which one party maintains prolonged electoral and institutional dominance, restricting the opposition's ability to compete on equal terms.

Institutional control

Party capture

The process through which a dominant political party appropriates state institutions and ostensibly independent organisations to reinforce its own political position.

Regime change

Creeping autocratization

The gradual erosion of democratic competition and institutional independence without necessarily eliminating elections or multiparty politics.

Electoral conditions

Political playing field

The institutional and electoral conditions determining whether competing political parties can participate in elections and political processes on relatively equal terms.

External engagement

Democracy support

International assistance aimed at strengthening democratic institutions, electoral competition, political participation and civil society.

Understanding dominant-party regimes

Dominant-party regimes differ from outright dictatorships because they retain electoral competition and some degree of political pluralism. Elections remain meaningful, but they are manifestly unfair.

How dominance is sustained
Institutional proximity Close relationships between the dominant party and executive institutions
→
Unequal resources Clientelist networks and disproportionate financial and institutional advantages
→
Unequal competition Opposition parties remain legal but cannot compete on equal terms

Dominant parties can effectively transform state institutions into extensions of their own political power. Their opponents may formally participate in elections, but the incumbent's institutional and financial advantages make competition structurally unequal.

Georgia as an illustration

The authors describe Georgian Dream's consolidation during its decade in power. They highlight its control over significant financial resources, clientelist networks, the justice system and electoral arrangements.

Why conventional democracy support fails

Traditional democracy assistance often focuses on strengthening state institutions, electoral commissions, civil society and administrative capacity. The problem is that dominant parties can capture or exploit these initiatives.

Institutional capture

Supporting captured institutions

Funding courts, parliaments and electoral authorities can inadvertently strengthen institutions already controlled by the incumbent.

Civil society limitations

Generic NGO support

Supporting NGOs does not necessarily produce an equal political playing field when the government retains control over significant political and economic resources.

Electoral assistance

Technical improvements alone

Improving the professionalism of electoral commissions has limited impact when dominant parties retain control over political patronage and electoral arrangements.

Underlying distinction

Capacity versus competition

Institutional capacity-building does not automatically strengthen democratic competition. The political conditions in which institutions operate matter.

Conventional focus

Institutional capacity

Improving the technical functioning and administrative capabilities of public institutions.

Authors' priority

Democratic competition

Changing the political conditions that determine whether opposition parties and civic actors can meaningfully challenge incumbent power.

Three policy priorities for democracy support

The authors identify three interconnected areas requiring greater attention from international donors.

01
Political parties and dialogue

Strengthen the capacity to compete

Donors should strengthen opposition parties, support multiparty dialogue, facilitate negotiations between incumbents and opposition forces, and protect threatened political activists. Dialogue alone is insufficient and must be complemented by direct party support.

02
Electoral reform

Address structural disadvantages

International actors should address disproportionate representation, electoral commission appointments and unequal campaign conditions. Assistance should begin immediately after elections rather than concentrating exclusively on election periods.

03
Civic mobilisation

Support direct activism

Donors should support grassroots mobilisation, protest movements, nontraditional organisations and local civic networks. Protests can become crucial avenues for democratic resistance when institutional channels are restricted.

The Georgian case

Georgia demonstrates that dominant-party dynamics can emerge even within relatively open political systems. The authors examine how electoral advantages, opposition mobilisation and external engagement interact.

Georgian Dream consolidates power

The party's dominance is supported by significant financial resources, clientelist networks, influence over the justice system and electoral arrangements.

Opposition challenges incumbent advantages

Opposition mobilisation and EU-mediated negotiations challenge the government's electoral and political advantages.

Incomplete implementation

The resulting agreement is only partially implemented, illustrating the limitations of dialogue when political commitments are not fully translated into practice.

Protest as democratic resistance

Protests periodically prevent a deeper authoritarian turn, demonstrating the importance of mobilisation beyond formal institutions.

Comparative scope

The article draws on four country cases to examine the challenges posed by dominant-party regimes to international democracy promotion.

Georgia

Illustrates dominant-party consolidation within a relatively open political environment, alongside opposition mobilisation and EU engagement.

Mozambique

One of the comparative cases used to examine how dominant-party systems complicate external democracy support.

Nicaragua

One of the comparative cases used to examine the relationship between incumbent dominance and international assistance.

Zimbabwe

One of the comparative cases used to examine the constraints dominant-party regimes place on democratic competition.

Core takeaway

Supporting democratic institutions is not necessarily equivalent to supporting democracy. In dominant-party regimes, ostensibly neutral institutional assistance can reinforce authoritarian tendencies when institutions are captured by incumbents. Democracy support must therefore become more politically sensitive and context-specific, prioritising genuine party competition, electoral reform and civic activism, alongside stronger diplomatic engagement and a greater willingness to take political risks.
