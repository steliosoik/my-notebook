Country cases · Georgia

Autocracy and Democracy in Georgia

Burkadze · *Autocracy and Democracy in Georgia: What Made the Difference?* · Chapter 1

Georgia's democratic trajectory between 1991 and 2020 cannot be understood exclusively through domestic political developments. It emerged through competition between Russia's authoritarian influence and Western democracy promotion, particularly by the United States, NATO and the European Union. The outcome depended not only on external resources and pressure, but also on domestic political agency and popular preferences.

Core concepts

External influence

External autocratizers

Foreign actors that use political, economic, military and societal influence to encourage or sustain authoritarian tendencies in another state.

External influence

External democratizers

International actors that promote democratic development through financial assistance, institutional cooperation, conditionality and political engagement.

Competition

Strategic rivalry

Competition between external powers that use different forms of leverage to influence another country's political orientation.

Institutional control

Imposed membership

The use of international organisations and institutional arrangements to constrain a state's foreign-policy choices and increase external influence.

Instruments

Political levers

Instruments of external influence, including political, human-based, energy, economic and military resources.

Two competing external orientations

The chapter presents Georgia as a site of strategic competition. Russia sought to constrain Georgia's Western orientation, while Western actors supported democratic development through different institutional and political instruments.

External autocratizer

Russia

Used strategic rivalry and imposed membership to retain regional influence, exploit dependencies and constrain Georgia's independent foreign-policy choices.

External democratizers

United States, EU and NATO

Provided political, financial, institutional and security support intended to strengthen democratic institutions and Georgia's Western integration.

Russia's instruments of leverage

Russia employed two principal mechanisms: strategic rivalry and imposed membership. These operated through several interconnected forms of influence.

Russian leverage
Political and human-based Exploiting ethnic and territorial divisions, maintaining influence through pro-Russian ministers, and supporting media outlets and NGOs promoting anti-Western narratives.
Energy and economic Control over energy infrastructure, supply disruptions, trade embargoes and restrictions on remittances created dependencies that could be exploited politically.
Military Russian military bases and purported peacekeeping operations increased Moscow's influence over internal conflicts, particularly in Abkhazia and South Ossetia.
Institutional CIS and CSTO membership constrained Georgia's ability to pursue independent foreign policy and Euro-Atlantic integration.

The 2008 Russo-Georgian War represented the culmination of this strategic confrontation. Russia sought to undermine Georgia's Western orientation and prevent the displacement of its regional influence.

Western democracy promotion

The United States and the EU developed complementary but institutionally different approaches. NATO provided a security framework alongside European integration.

United States

Strategic partnership and assistance

Emphasised financial assistance, elections, political parties, NGOs, independent media and institutional capacity-building.

Scale: USAID invested approximately $1.8 billion in Georgia between 1992 and 2018.

European Union

Conditionality and integration

Relied more heavily on conditionality, institutional cooperation, economic integration, capacity-building and incentives for political reform.

Examples: Eastern Partnership, Association Agreement and visa liberalisation.

NATO

Security framework

Provided a security framework complementary to European integration. Disagreement between European and American governments prevented Georgia from receiving a Membership Action Plan at the 2008 Bucharest Summit.

Domestic agency and popular support

External assistance alone could not guarantee democratisation. Georgian political elites needed to translate international resources into domestic reforms. Equally important, public support for Western integration imposed electoral costs on politicians contemplating closer accommodation with Russia.

The Georgian trajectory

1991 · Independence

Georgia's post-Soviet political trajectory begins amid competing external pressures and domestic state-building challenges.

1992–2018 · Western assistance

USAID invests approximately $1.8 billion in Georgia, supporting elections, civil society, media and institutional capacity-building.

2008 · Russo-Georgian War

The culmination of strategic confrontation, as Russia seeks to undermine Georgia's Western orientation.

2008 · Bucharest Summit

Disagreement among NATO members prevents Georgia from receiving a Membership Action Plan.

2014 onward · European integration

The EU's Association Agreement and visa liberalisation exemplify its approach through conditionality and institutional integration.

2020 · End of the period examined

The chapter's analysis covers the interaction of external competition, Western support and domestic political agency through 2020.

Core takeaway

Georgia's democratisation resulted from the interaction between external geopolitical competition, Western institutional support, domestic political agency and popular pro-Western preferences. Great-power competition ultimately proved beneficial for Georgian democratisation: Russian pressure encouraged closer Western alignment, while American and European engagement provided resources and incentives for institutional reform. External democracy promotion was more effective when reinforced by domestic public support.
