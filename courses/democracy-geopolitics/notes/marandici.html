Democracy & Geopolitics / Moldova / Session 9

Taming the Oligarchs?

Democratization and state capture: the case of Moldova

Ion Marandici 2021 Demokratizatsiya, 29(1), 61–88 Publication details
Oligarchy State capture Democratization Grand corruption Collective action Deoligarchization
The argument in one minute

Oligarchs can help defeat authoritarianism while simultaneously undermining democracy.

Marandici argues that Moldova's oligarchs initially helped dismantle competitive authoritarianism by building rival political camps and challenging the Communist government. Once in power, however, they used extensive networks to capture state institutions and extract public resources. The resulting corruption provoked a delayed, cross-ideological popular backlash. This helped produce partial deoligarchization, but not the full restoration of state autonomy or democratic consolidation.

01

The central problem: oligarchy and democratization

Marandici challenges approaches to democratization that concentrate primarily on political parties, formal institutions and electoral competition. These approaches often overlook the political influence of extremely wealthy individuals whose resources allow them to shape the state from outside formal office.

The Moldovan case exposes a paradox. Oligarchs were instrumental in ending the competitive authoritarian regime, but their subsequent domination of the state obstructed democratic consolidation. Democratization and state capture therefore did not simply represent opposing processes. In Moldova, they initially developed alongside one another.

CORE CONCEPT 01

Oligarchic state capture

The temporary domination of the state by one or several oligarchic networks, which exercise disproportionate influence over state institutions through extensive connections.

CORE CONCEPT 02

State autonomy

The capacity of the state to formulate and pursue goals that are not simply reflective of the demands or interests of wealthy social groups.

CORE CONCEPT 03

State exploitation

The appropriation of state resources by political actors for private or political benefit. In Moldova, this developed into large-scale extraction through oligarchic networks.

CORE CONCEPT 04

Deoligarchization

The removal of oligarchs from politics and the reduction of their control over the state. Marandici distinguishes partial deoligarchization from successful democratic consolidation.

The article adopts a state-centered perspective. Drawing on Jeffrey Winters and Theda Skocpol, Marandici treats the state as potentially autonomous from wealthy elites rather than automatically reflecting the interests of the capitalist class. The central question becomes how that autonomy is lost, and under what conditions it can be recovered.

02

The five sequences of oligarchic state capture

The article's central explanatory contribution is its comparative sequential account of Moldova between 2009 and 2019. Rather than treating oligarchic influence as static, Marandici traces how the relationship between oligarchs, the state and collective action changed over time.

THE POLITICAL TRAJECTORY, 2009–2019
01 2009

Oligarchs defeat competitive authoritarianism

Vlad Filat builds the Liberal Democratic Party (PLDM) into a serious challenger to Vladimir Voronin's Communist regime. Following the 2009 electoral crisis and protests, Vlad Plahotniuc enters the political arena by sponsoring the Democratic Party (PDM). Their parties join the Alliance for European Integration (AIE), displacing the Communists.

OUTCOME: THE END OF COMPETITIVE AUTHORITARIANISM
02 2009–15

State capture by two oligarchs

Filat and Plahotniuc compete over political influence, assets and state resources. Their networks penetrate government institutions, distribute public positions through patronage and weaken the capacity of the bureaucracy and judiciary to constrain them. Filat's network initially dominates state exploitation.

OUTCOME: OLIGARCHIC CONTROL AND GRAND CORRUPTION
03 2015

Collective action amid inter-oligarchic wars

The banking scandal exposes the scale of state exploitation. Filat's arrest, enabled partly by conflict between oligarchic networks, coincides with growing public mobilization. Anti-oligarchic politics becomes a prominent issue across ideological and ethnolinguistic divisions.

OUTCOME: ANTI-OLIGARCHIC MOBILIZATION
04 2015–19

Collective action against Plahotniuc's dominance

Following Filat's removal, Plahotniuc becomes the dominant powerbroker. Opposition groups and protest movements increasingly target his informal control over the state. New political forces emerge from the protest movement, eventually forming the ACUM alliance.

OUTCOME: A BROADER ANTI-OLIGARCHIC COALITION
05 2019

Incomplete deoligarchization

ACUM and the Socialists form a temporary governing coalition in June 2019. Plahotniuc leaves the country and his network begins to disintegrate. Yet institutional vulnerabilities, political opportunism and populist alternatives persist.

OUTCOME: OLIGARCHIC REMOVAL WITHOUT FULL DEMOCRATIC CONSOLIDATION

The sequences are causally ordered: each consists of multiple events and processes that help produce the next stage. The analytical point is that oligarchic power changes form over time. Its initial contribution to political competition does not prevent it from subsequently undermining democracy.

03

From democratizers to predators

Marandici's argument is not that oligarchs are inherently anti-democratic in every political context. Their relationship with democracy is contingent on their position within the political system and the incentives they face.

THE SHIFT IN OLIGARCHIC INCENTIVES
PHASE 01 Opposition to competitive authoritarianism

Rival oligarchs build political camps and challenge an incumbent regime that restricts their opportunities.

→
PHASE 02 Access to state power

Political competition opens access to government, public resources and institutional influence.

→
PHASE 03 State exploitation

Oligarchic networks use the state for extraction, patronage and electoral advantage.

The disappearance of the common authoritarian threat changes the incentives of oligarchs. Rather than continuing to support democratic consolidation, they increasingly seek to control and exploit the institutions that emerged from the transition.

This challenges the assumption that oligarchic political involvement is primarily a strategy of wealth defense. Marandici finds that Moldova's oligarchs engaged in extreme state exploitation: they constructed patronage networks that extracted public resources for personal enrichment and electoral purposes.

These networks were multifunctional. Beyond extracting resources, they could mobilize citizens, spread disinformation and administer violence. Oligarchic power therefore extended beyond financial influence into the organization of political life itself.

Why political competition was insufficient

Marandici also challenges the idea that electoral competition necessarily moderates state capture. In Moldova, political competition was structured around identity, regional and geopolitical cleavages rather than consistently around good governance.

Parties sponsored by oligarchs could mobilize voters through these established divisions. Once in government, they could exploit the state while maintaining political support. Competition could therefore enable capture before it eventually generated opposition to it.

04

The “robbery of the century”

The theft of more than one billion US dollars from three Moldovan banks is the article's central empirical illustration of extreme state exploitation. The fraud involved Banca de Economii (BEM), Banca Socială and Unibank. The stolen amount was equivalent to approximately 12% of Moldova's GDP.

12% Approximate share of national GDP
3 banks BEM, Banca Socială and Unibank

How the fraud operated

A simplified account of the mechanisms described by Marandici

1
Acquisition and control of the banks

Oligarchic groups competed over BEM, a systemic bank through which the state disbursed substantial public funds. Shares were acquired through intermediaries and politically connected transactions.

2
Loans to connected companies

The banks issued large loans to politically connected firms and fictitious businesses, often with dubious collateral. Funds moved through offshore companies and international banking channels.

3
Concealment and artificial financial stability

Complex transfers obscured the origin and destination of funds. Some transactions created the appearance of legitimate business activity and concealed the banks' deteriorating financial position.

4
Government bailout

The government injected public funds to prevent a banking collapse. The Leancă and Gaburici governments ultimately injected more than one billion dollars into the three banks.

5
Public absorption of the losses

The banks were eventually renationalized and liquidated. The financial consequences of the fraud were transferred to the state, while public confidence in political institutions deteriorated.

Why this mattered politically

The fraud demonstrated that oligarchic networks could exploit public institutions on a massive scale while relying on the government to absorb the consequences. It transformed corruption from an abstract political accusation into a visible issue of public loss and institutional betrayal.

The political consequences

The banking fraud exposed the relationship between oligarchic networks and the state. It also revealed the weakness of regulatory oversight and the inability of captured institutions to prevent the extraction of public resources.

The bailout was especially damaging because it demonstrated that the government was not simply failing to prevent private wrongdoing. Public authority itself was being used to sustain the consequences of the fraud.

The result was a dramatic decline in trust in democratic institutions. In October 2015, 89% of Moldovans expressed dissatisfaction with democracy. Trust in Parliament fell from 24% in 2014 to just 6%.

05

Collective action and the anti-oligarchic coalition

The backlash against state capture developed after the banking fraud, not before it. Marandici emphasizes that collective action was a delayed response to extreme exploitation rather than a permanent or pre-existing societal constraint on oligarchic power.

From fragmented opposition to shared grievance

Initially, opposition to the oligarchs was fragmented. The Communist Party (PCRM) adopted anti-oligarchic rhetoric, but its appeals relied heavily on Soviet nostalgia and fears of a possible union with Romania. These appeals did not produce a broad coalition.

The banking fraud changed the political environment. Disillusioned center-right voters appropriated the anti-oligarchic theme, which had previously been associated with the left. Protests intensified during 2015 and culminated in the occupation of two major squares in Chișinău.

CENTER-RIGHT PROTEST CAMP

Freedom and Dignity City

Established by center-right demonstrators, including veterans of the Transnistrian conflict and supporters of unionist positions. The movement helped generate the Dignity and Truth Platform and Action and Solidarity Party.

LEFT AND POPULIST PROTEST CAMP

Victory Town

Organized around the Party of Socialists and Our Party. It expressed opposition to oligarchic rule from a different ideological and geopolitical position.

Although the protest camps initially remained separate, both demanded accountability for the banking fraud. Their common opposition to oligarchic influence eventually enabled joint demonstrations.

The result was unusual in Moldovan politics: a multiethnic, transideological anti-oligarchic coalition that crossed divisions which had traditionally structured the party system.

From protest to political organization

The protest movement also generated new political actors. The Dignity and Truth Platform (PDA) and Action and Solidarity Party (PAS) emerged from the center-right mobilization. They later joined forces in the ACUM alliance, which contested elections against parties identified with oligarchic interests.

Marandici's point is that collective action helped turn state capture into a politically salient issue. It created a constituency that demanded not only the removal of individual oligarchs, but also impartial judges, independent prosecutors and institutional reforms.

06

Plahotniuc's dominance and the limits of resistance

Filat's arrest did not end oligarchic state capture. Instead, it transformed its configuration. Plahotniuc emerged as the dominant powerbroker, expanding his network of political allies and institutional influence.

Vlad Plahotniuc

Informal powerbroker

Plahotniuc's influence depended less on occupying the highest formal office than on controlling the relationships and networks through which political decisions were made. Although he attempted to become prime minister, President Nicolae Timofti rejected his nomination on integrity grounds.

He instead relied on an extensive informal network. The Democratic Party participated in every governing coalition between 2009 and 2019, while Plahotniuc repeatedly avoided holding a parliamentary seat himself.

His influence extended into the judiciary. After Andrei Năstase won the Chișinău mayoral election, judges annulled the result on questionable grounds. The episode prompted EU officials to describe Moldova as a captured state.

What this reveals

Formal political office is not a reliable measure of where power is located. Informal networks can dominate decision-making while their central actors remain outside the most visible state positions.

The international dimension

External actors played an ambiguous role in the process. Western governments and organizations initially supported the AIE government and its pro-European orientation. Filat and Plahotniuc cultivated relationships with Western officials and lobbying firms, gaining international legitimacy despite their domestic practices.

Marandici argues that external actors increasingly opposed oligarchic influence only after domestic collective action reached a tipping point. Their role was therefore neither consistently supportive of democratization nor uniformly opposed to oligarchic capture.

Plahotniuc also pursued policies critical of Russia, including measures concerning Russian military personnel and the Transnistrian conflict. This complicated the relationship between geopolitical alignment and democratic legitimacy: a pro-Western orientation did not necessarily indicate an autonomous or democratic state.

07

2019: partial deoligarchization

The 2019 parliamentary elections created an opportunity to challenge Plahotniuc's dominance. ACUM and the Party of Socialists obtained sufficient parliamentary support to form a government. Despite their ideological differences, they agreed on a temporary political arrangement to deoligarchize the state.

The coalition was backed by the United States, the European Union and Russia. Its formation marked a significant moment because political actors with competing geopolitical and ideological orientations converged around opposition to oligarchic state capture.

The collapse of Plahotniuc's network

Plahotniuc's network initially resisted the new government. A period of dual power followed, with competing claims to governmental legitimacy and the mobilization of political supporters. Under pressure from domestic opposition and external actors, Plahotniuc eventually left Moldova for the United States.

His departure triggered the disintegration of parts of his network. Government officials, Constitutional Court members and law enforcement figures resigned. Some oligarchic assets were seized, although media and party structures associated with the network continued to operate.

Why the process remained incomplete

The removal of Plahotniuc did not automatically produce democratic consolidation. The coalition between ACUM and the Socialists was fragile, and the parties eventually fell out. The Socialists subsequently allied with the Democratic Party and voted out the ACUM government.

The political space left by the departing oligarchs also created opportunities for populist and Eurosceptic forces, including the Șor Party. These actors did not necessarily represent a democratic alternative to oligarchic rule.

Do not conflate deoligarchization with democratization

The removal of oligarchs from politics did not guarantee the recovery of stolen resources, the creation of autonomous institutions or the consolidation of liberal democracy. Deoligarchization was real but incomplete, and its political consequences were mixed.

08

Theoretical and policy implications

Marandici's analysis challenges three assumptions in the literature on state capture and democratization.

1. Oligarchs are not static political actors

Their relationship with democracy changes over time. They can initially contribute to the defeat of competitive authoritarianism and subsequently undermine democratic consolidation once their interests are better served by controlling the state.

2. State capture is more than wealth defense

Oligarchs do not simply seek to protect existing assets. They can actively exploit public institutions to extract resources, finance political networks and preserve electoral influence. Their power is therefore both economic and political.

3. Institutional reform requires a social constituency

Collective action against oligarchic capture emerges after extensive exploitation has already occurred. Protest helps create a constituency that supports independent law enforcement and anti-corruption institutions.

Consequently, institutional redesign alone is unlikely to resolve oligarchic state capture. Reforms require political support from civil society activists and political elites committed to good governance.

Effective deoligarchization therefore depends on collective action combined with the creation of pockets of state autonomy: institutions sufficiently insulated from the particularistic pressures of oligarchic networks and electoral competition.

The central takeaway

Moldova demonstrates that oligarchic power can both facilitate and obstruct democratization. Oligarchs helped defeat competitive authoritarianism, but subsequently captured the state and used it for large-scale extraction. The resulting popular backlash enabled a diverse coalition to challenge their dominance. Yet removing oligarchs was not equivalent to restoring state autonomy or consolidating democracy. Durable deoligarchization requires collective action and institutions capable of operating independently of wealthy elites.
