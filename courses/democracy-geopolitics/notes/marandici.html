DEMOCRACY & GEOPOLITICS / MOLDOVA

Taming the Oligarchs?

Democratization and state capture: the case of Moldova

ION MARANDICI · 2021
Demokratizatsiya: The Journal of Post-Soviet Democratization, 29(1), 61–88
Publication: Winter 2021
Oligarchy State capture Democratisation State autonomy Collective action

1. Overview and central argument

Marandici examines the relationship between oligarchic state capture and democratisation in Moldova between 2009 and 2019. He argues that oligarchs initially helped bring down competitive authoritarianism, but subsequently obstructed democratic consolidation by exploiting the state for private and political gain.

The article challenges explanations that focus primarily on institutional design, party competition or the protection of private property. Instead, it emphasises the autonomy of the state, the role of wealthy business magnates and the capacity of collective action to challenge oligarchic dominance.

2. Core concepts

Oligarchic state capture: The temporary loss of state autonomy vis-à-vis wealthy elites, whose networks influence the branches of government and pursue wealth defence, resource extraction and state exploitation.
State autonomy: The capacity of public institutions and officials to act independently of particularistic pressures from wealthy elites and oligarchic networks.
State exploitation: The extraction and appropriation of public resources by political and economic actors for private enrichment or political purposes.
Deoligarchization: The process of reducing oligarchic influence over the state and restoring institutional autonomy. Marandici distinguishes partial deoligarchization from deeper democratic consolidation.
Collective action: The mobilisation of citizens, civil society actors and political elites around opposition to oligarchic domination and demands for autonomous public institutions.

3. The trajectory of oligarchic capture (2009–2019)

From competitive authoritarianism to oligarchic influence

Following the 2009 political transition, oligarchic actors became important in challenging the Communist Party's competitive authoritarian rule. However, the defeat of the old regime did not remove oligarchic power. Instead, influential business figures gained leverage over political parties, state institutions and decision-making networks.

Marandici's central paradox is that oligarchs could act as agents of political change while simultaneously undermining the democratic order that followed. Their cooperation against competitive authoritarianism did not reflect a durable commitment to democratic governance.

State exploitation and the banking fraud

The most consequential example was the 2014 banking fraud, commonly known as the “theft of the century”. More than one billion US dollars was extracted from three Moldovan banks, a sum equivalent to approximately 12% of GDP. The scandal exposed the depth of oligarchic influence and the vulnerability of public institutions.

Rather than simply protecting existing wealth, oligarchic networks engaged in extensive exploitation of the state. The consequences included weakened public trust, institutional discredit and growing popular anger.

Collective action and anti-oligarchic mobilisation

The banking scandal helped transform state capture into a central political issue. Protests in 2015 brought together actors from different ideological and ethnolinguistic backgrounds. Anti-oligarchic mobilisation crossed the divisions that had traditionally structured Moldovan politics.

The subsequent political realignment contributed to the emergence of ACUM, an alliance involving the Dignity and Truth Platform and the Party of Action and Solidarity (PAS). This coalition represented a broader challenge to oligarchic dominance.

4. The 2019 transition and its limits

In 2019, ACUM and the Socialist Party formed a temporary coalition to remove Vlad Plahotniuc's Democratic Party network from power. Inter-oligarchic conflict, public mobilisation and political cooperation created an opening for partial deoligarchization.

However, the removal of Plahotniuc's network did not amount to complete institutional transformation. Oligarchic assets, media influence and political structures persisted, while the coalition itself proved fragile and eventually collapsed.

Marandici therefore distinguishes between removing a dominant oligarch and restoring state autonomy. Political turnover can disrupt capture without dismantling the networks and institutional dependencies that sustain it.

5. What makes deoligarchization effective?

The article argues that institutional reform alone is insufficient. Effective deoligarchization requires a combination of collective action and political support for autonomous state institutions.

  • Collective action: A broad constituency must sustain opposition to oligarchic influence beyond a single electoral or political confrontation.
  • Autonomous institutions: Law enforcement and anti-corruption agencies need insulation from oligarchic pressure and political interference.
  • Committed political elites: Reform requires political actors willing to support state autonomy and the rule of law.
  • Public accountability: Citizens and civil society must be able to monitor state resources and challenge predatory networks.

The central lesson is that democratic consolidation depends not simply on changing political leaders, but on rebuilding the state's capacity to act independently of wealthy private interests.

Key takeaway
Oligarchs can help dismantle competitive authoritarianism while subsequently undermining democratisation through state exploitation. Deoligarchization therefore requires more than replacing dominant elites: it depends on collective action and the construction of durable pockets of state autonomy.

Source: Marandici, I. (2021). “Taming the Oligarchs? Democratization and State Capture: The Case of Moldova.” Demokratizatsiya: The Journal of Post-Soviet Democratization, 29(1), 61–88.
