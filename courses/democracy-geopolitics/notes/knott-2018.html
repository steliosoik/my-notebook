Democracy & Geopolitics / Moldova / Session 9

Perpetually “Partly Free”

Lessons from post-Soviet hybrid regimes on backsliding in Central and Eastern Europe

Eleanor Knott 2018 East European Politics, 34(3), 355–376 Read publication ↗
Hybrid regimes Democratic backsliding Informal politics State capture Media capture Collective mobilisation
The argument in one minute

Why do some countries remain caught between democracy and authoritarianism?

Knott argues that hybrid regimes persist because two forces operate simultaneously. Business-political networks, state capture and media capture obstruct democratisation. Civil society and citizens' capacity to mobilise, meanwhile, constrain authoritarian consolidation. The result is not necessarily a transition in either direction, but a recurring democratic-authoritarian equilibrium.

01

Starting point: beyond a linear transition

Knott begins by challenging the assumption that political systems necessarily move along a linear path from authoritarianism towards democracy, or from democracy towards authoritarianism. Moldova and Ukraine have spent much of the post-Soviet period in a political grey zone, experiencing alternating episodes of democratic progress and backsliding without consolidating either regime type.

The article calls this condition a democratic-authoritarian dynamic equilibrium. It is important that this is not simply a transitional stage. The equilibrium can become durable because the political arrangements sustaining it benefit actors with influence, while also making meaningful reform difficult.

THE TWO FORCES SUSTAINING HYBRIDITY
Restrains democratisation

Informal elite power

State capture, media capture, corruption and patron-client networks preserve an uneven political playing field.

Restrains authoritarianism

Societal mobilisation

Civil society, protest movements and citizens' capacity to organise make complete authoritarian consolidation harder.

Democratic-authoritarian dynamic equilibrium

The important point is the interaction between these forces. Democratic progress is repeatedly obstructed, but attempts to monopolise power are also challenged. Neither side necessarily wins decisively.

02

The analytical framework

Knott uses hybrid regimes, particularly the competitive authoritarianism framework associated with Levitsky and Way, to move beyond a binary distinction between democracy and authoritarianism. Elections and political competition exist, but they are not necessarily fair: incumbents can manipulate institutions, resources and media to maintain an uneven playing field.

CONCEPT 01

Hybrid regimes

Systems combining formal democratic institutions and multiparty elections with persistent violations of political rights, civil liberties and electoral fairness.

CONCEPT 02

Backsliding

Gradual democratic erosion through the weakening of political competition and protections. Knott uses the term beyond consolidated democracies.

CONCEPT 03

Extra-incumbent factors

Informal power held outside the presidency and formal political office, especially by business-political elites who can influence state institutions.

CONCEPT 04

Informal politics

Political influence exercised through patron-client relationships, personal networks and arrangements that operate beyond formal institutional rules.

This is the article's main analytical move: explaining backsliding requires looking beyond presidents, parties and formal institutions. Knott directs attention towards actors who may not hold office but can nevertheless shape the conditions under which political competition takes place.

03

Three mechanisms that shape the equilibrium

A. State capture and crony capitalism

Systemic corruption and business-political networks make the state a resource for enrichment. Rather than treating corruption as a collection of isolated transactions, Knott presents it as part of a political system that reproduces itself.

Weak judiciaries, police and representative institutions create opportunities for powerful actors to exploit public authority. Those who benefit from these arrangements have few incentives to strengthen the institutions that could constrain them.

B. Media capture and the uneven playing field

Media ownership gives business-political elites the capacity to shape public debate, influence electoral competition and weaken opposition visibility. Media capture is analytically distinct from state capture, but it can enable and reinforce it.

This matters because political competition can remain formally open while becoming substantively unequal. Control over the information environment can favour incumbents and their informal allies without the need to abolish elections.

C. Civil society and citizen mobilisation

The countervailing force is the capacity of citizens to organise, protest and challenge political elites. Mobilisation can expose corruption, disrupt elite strategies and make authoritarian consolidation more costly.

Knott does not treat civil society as an automatic engine of democratisation. Protest can constrain authoritarian power without dismantling the structures that obstruct democratic consolidation.

A distinction worth keeping in mind

Mobilisation can prevent authoritarian consolidation without producing consolidated democracy. The capacity to challenge power and the capacity to build autonomous institutions are not the same thing.

04

Moldova: concentrated informal power

Moldova

State and media capture

Moldova illustrates how informal business-political networks can exercise influence far beyond formal political office. Knott focuses particularly on Vlad Plahotniuc, whose network combined political influence with extensive media ownership.

The article notes that Plahotniuc owned four of the country's five major television stations, as well as three major radio stations. This concentration enabled the shaping of public debate and the marginalisation of political rivals.

The case demonstrates why analysing the government alone is insufficient. Informal networks can exercise decisive influence over the political playing field even when they do not occupy the most visible institutional positions.

What Moldova demonstrates

A concentrated business-political network can combine institutional influence and media ownership to make democratic competition formally present but substantively unequal.

05

Ukraine: pluralism without independence

Ukraine

Rival oligarchic networks

Ukraine also experienced extensive oligarchic influence over media and politics, but its media environment was more pluralistic because competing business elites owned rival outlets. This created a different configuration from Moldova's concentrated media ownership.

However, pluralism did not mean independence. Media outlets remained politically instrumentalised by their owners. Competition between oligarchs could create space for competing narratives without producing an autonomous public sphere.

What Ukraine demonstrates

Rival ownership can limit the dominance of one political network, but a plural media market is not necessarily an independent media system.

Mobilisation as a counterweight

Ukraine also illustrates the capacity of popular mobilisation to interrupt authoritarian projects. The Orange Revolution demonstrated that protest could make electoral outcomes consequential and challenge incumbent dominance.

Under Viktor Yanukovych, backsliding took the form of constitutional changes, increased presidential power and coercive practices. Euromaidan began in late 2013 as a pro-European protest, but intensified repression and violence transformed it into the Revolution of Dignity, with a broader anti-corruption and anti-system character.

Yet the aftermath also illustrates the limits of mobilisation: removing an incumbent and opening political space does not automatically resolve institutional weaknesses or guarantee the completion of reform.

06

Comparative logic: what the cases reveal

Knott does not present Moldova and Ukraine primarily as a controlled comparison designed to explain differences between the two countries. Rather, both are illustrative cases of the dynamic equilibrium characteristic of post-Soviet hybrid regimes.

Moldova

Concentrated media ownership and Plahotniuc's extensive informal network illustrate how a dominant business-political actor can shape the political playing field.

Ukraine

Rival oligarchic media owners create a more pluralistic but still politicised media environment. Protest provides an important additional constraint on incumbents.

The broader lesson is that the precise configuration of informal power may differ, but the relationship between elite capture and democratic vulnerability remains central. Ethnic and geopolitical divisions can also obscure these informal relationships. Knott cautions against allowing visible identity cleavages to distract from the political networks that sustain enrichment-based politics.

07

Wider implications for Central and Eastern Europe

Knott uses post-Soviet experiences to identify risks for more consolidated Central and Eastern European democracies. Backsliding does not necessarily lead directly to authoritarianism. It can instead produce a prolonged hybrid condition in which elections continue but informal elite networks undermine meaningful democratic accountability.

  • Look beyond incumbents. Political power may be exercised by business-political networks outside formal office.
  • Distinguish media pluralism from independence. Competition between politically controlled outlets does not necessarily create a free public sphere.
  • Do not equate protest with democratisation. Mobilisation can disrupt authoritarian consolidation without producing institutional autonomy.
  • Recognise the durability of hybridity. Hybrid regimes are not necessarily temporary stages on the way to democracy or authoritarianism.

The equilibrium can be difficult to overcome because the actors who benefit from informal political arrangements have limited incentives to reform them. Even actors seeking to challenge the system may become embedded in its networks and practices.

The central takeaway

Hybrid regimes endure through the interaction of two forces: informal business-political power obstructs democratisation, while civil society mobilisation constrains authoritarian consolidation. The result is a durable political equilibrium in which elections and protest remain possible, but neither necessarily produces democratic consolidation.
