DEMOCRACY & GEOPOLITICS / MOLDOVA

Perpetually “Partly Free”

Lessons from post-Soviet hybrid regimes on backsliding in Central and Eastern Europe

ELEANOR KNOTT · 2018
East European Politics, 34(3), 355–376
DOI: 10.1080/21599165.2018.1493993
Hybrid regimes Democratic backsliding State capture Media capture Civil society

1. Overview and central argument

Knott examines Moldova and Ukraine to explain why some post-Soviet states have remained in a prolonged condition between democracy and authoritarianism. Rather than following a linear transition towards either regime type, these countries experience recurring periods of democratisation and backsliding. Knott conceptualises this condition as a democratic-authoritarian dynamic equilibrium.

The article argues that this equilibrium cannot be explained solely through the actions of presidents or governing parties. Informal power held by business-political elites, particularly through state and media capture, obstructs democratic consolidation. At the same time, civil society and citizens' capacity to mobilise constrain authoritarian consolidation.

2. Core concepts

Hybrid regimes: Political systems combining formal democratic institutions and multiparty elections with persistent violations of political rights, civil liberties and electoral fairness.
Democratic-authoritarian dynamic equilibrium: A condition in which democratisation and backsliding repeatedly alternate, preventing movement towards either consolidated democracy or consolidated authoritarianism.
Backsliding: The gradual erosion of political competition and democratic protections, often initiated by political elites. It can occur in regimes that have never achieved consolidated democracy.
Extra-incumbent factors: Informal sources of political power located outside the presidency and formal government, particularly business-political networks capable of influencing state institutions and public debate.
State and media capture: The use of political and economic influence to subordinate public institutions and media organisations to private or partisan interests.

3. Why democratisation remains limited

Extra-incumbent power and informal politics

Knott shifts attention away from formal political office towards informal networks and patron-client relationships. Business-political elites, sometimes described as “grey cardinals”, can exercise substantial influence without holding executive office. Their networks penetrate institutions and preserve opportunities for enrichment, limiting the autonomy of the state.

This perspective also cautions against treating ethnic or geopolitical cleavages as sufficient explanations for political outcomes. Such divisions can obscure the informal economic and political relationships that sustain state capture.

Media capture and unequal competition

Control over media allows powerful business-political actors to shape public debate, restrict pluralism and influence electoral competition. In Moldova, Vlad Plahotniuc's network controlled a substantial share of the broadcasting market, illustrating how media concentration can reinforce political dominance beyond formal government positions.

Ukraine's media environment was more pluralistic because competing oligarchic groups controlled different outlets. However, this pluralism did not make the media independent: outlets remained politically instrumentalised by their owners.

4. Why authoritarian consolidation is also constrained

Mobilisation and civil society

Citizens and civil society organisations can act as checks on authoritarian consolidation. Protest movements may challenge incumbents, expose corruption and demonstrate that political authority remains contestable.

Ukraine's Orange Revolution and Euromaidan illustrate how mobilisation can disrupt incumbent dominance. Euromaidan began as a pro-European protest and developed into a broader anti-corruption and anti-system mobilisation amid state repression and violence.

Limits of mobilisation

Mobilisation does not automatically produce democratic consolidation. Protest movements can remove incumbents or interrupt authoritarian projects without dismantling the informal networks and institutional weaknesses that enabled them. Social media can facilitate information circulation, but mobilisation also depends heavily on informal personal networks. Civil society itself should not automatically be assumed to be liberal or consistently democratic.

5. Comparative implications

Knott uses Moldova and Ukraine to draw lessons for Central and Eastern European states experiencing democratic backsliding. Democratic erosion need not culminate in consolidated authoritarianism. Instead, extra-incumbent networks, state capture and media capture can sustain a prolonged hybrid condition.

The central analytical implication is that examining elections and incumbent behaviour alone produces an incomplete picture. Understanding democratic resilience and backsliding requires attention to informal power, ownership structures, institutional autonomy and the capacity for collective mobilisation.

Key takeaway
Hybrid regimes persist because democratisation is obstructed by informal business-political power, state capture and media capture, while authoritarian consolidation is checked by citizens' capacity to mobilise. Neither electoral competition nor protest alone guarantees democratic consolidation.

Source: Knott, E. (2018). “Perpetually ‘partly free’: Lessons from post-Soviet hybrid regimes on backsliding in Central and Eastern Europe.” East European Politics, 34(3), 355–376.
