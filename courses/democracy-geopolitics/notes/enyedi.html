DEMOCRACY & GEOPOLITICS / SESSION 4

The Role of Agency in Cleavage Formation

How political actors construct and reshape political divisions

Zsolt Enyedi (2005)

CENTRAL ARGUMENT

Political cleavages do not simply reflect pre-existing social divisions. Parties and political entrepreneurs actively shape how social differences become politically meaningful, combining separate constituencies into broader political identities. However, their capacity to do so remains constrained by existing social structures and institutional conditions.

Core concepts

POLITICAL STRUCTURE

Political cleavage

A durable pattern of political competition embedded in social, cognitive or emotional structures, rather than temporary reactions to particular issues or personalities.

AGENCY

Political agency

The capacity of political actors, particularly parties and their leaders, to shape how social differences become politically meaningful.

ACTORS

Political entrepreneurs

Actors who mobilise interests and identities, construct alliances and organise political competition.

PARTY ORGANISATION

Mosaic cleavage party

A party that integrates several previously separate social groups under a common political identity.

INSTITUTIONS

Institutional thresholds

Electoral and institutional conditions that encourage parties to build sufficiently large coalitions to gain political power.

1. From social differences to political cleavages

Traditional cleavage analysis often examines correlations between characteristics such as class, religion and geography and voting behaviour. Enyedi argues that this misses the political process through which those differences acquire meaning.

01 · SOCIETY

Social differences

Class, religion, geography and other existing social characteristics.

02 · INTERPRETATION

Political framing

Political actors define which differences matter and how they should be understood.

03 · MOBILISATION

Coalition-building

Previously separate groups are encouraged to identify with one another.

04 · ORGANISATION

Political identity

Parties consolidate shared identities and organise political competition.

05 · OUTCOME

Cleavage formation

A more durable pattern of political competition emerges.

Analytical distinction: The existence of social differences does not automatically produce political conflict. Political actors help determine which differences become politically salient and how separate groups come to identify with a common political camp.

2. The three forces shaping cleavage formation

Parties operate between existing social conditions, political agency and institutional constraints. They can mobilise existing divisions, but they can also construct new political identities and alliances.

◉

Social and attitudinal structures

Existing social characteristics, interests and attitudes provide the material from which political identities can be constructed.

◇

Political entrepreneurs

Parties and their leaders mobilise groups, construct common identities and reorganise political competition.

▦

Institutional constraints

Electoral systems and the pursuit of political power encourage coalition-building and influence the strategies available to parties.

Agency is consequential, but not unlimited. Political actors work with existing social structures and institutional conditions rather than operating independently of them.

3. Mosaic cleavage parties

A mosaic cleavage party brings together several previously separate social constituencies around a common ideological denominator.

German CDU

The integration of Catholic and Protestant voters illustrates how a party can bring distinct religious constituencies together within a broader political identity.

Hungarian Fidesz

Enyedi argues that Fidesz underwent a comparable transformation, reorganising political competition and bringing different constituencies into a broader conservative camp.

4. Fidesz and the construction of a political camp

The Hungarian case illustrates how ideological repositioning and organisational development can reshape the relationship between social characteristics and party preference.

1988 · FOUNDING

A liberal, anti-Communist youth movement

Fidesz emerges as a liberal youth movement opposing Communist rule.

1992–1994 · ELECTORAL DECLINE

A strategic ideological repositioning

Despite an electoral decline from approximately 33% in 1992 to 7% in 1994, the leadership pursues a longer-term strategy of ideological and organisational consolidation, moving towards Christian-nationalist conservatism.

ORGANISATIONAL EXPANSION

Building a wider conservative environment

Fidesz cultivates connections with conservative intellectuals, religious organisations and civic associations, extending its presence beyond electoral politics.

2002 · ELECTORAL DEFEAT

The Civic Circles

Following its electoral defeat, the Civic Circles further expand the party's organisational presence and create a wider social environment around it.

1994–2002 · ELECTORATE TRANSFORMATION

A changing relationship between society and voting

Enyedi's analysis finds that religiosity, urbanisation, left-right identification and authoritarian attitudes became differently associated with party preference as Fidesz changed its political orientation.

5. Evidence of changing political identities

Enyedi's analysis of Hungarian electorates between 1994 and 2002 suggests that party constituencies and the political meaning of social characteristics can change together.

1994–2002

The period examined in the analysis of changing relationships between social characteristics, attitudes and party preference.

Age × authoritarianism

The relationship between age and authoritarianism weakened considerably, suggesting that political attitudes and identities are not entirely fixed by social background.

The changing associations between religiosity, urbanisation, left-right identification, authoritarian attitudes and party preference support the argument that political identities are shaped through political processes rather than simply inherited from society.

Methodological and analytical contribution

Enyedi develops a conceptual argument about the role of political agency in cleavage formation, using the transformation of Fidesz and the changing Hungarian electorate as the central empirical illustration. The analysis demonstrates how party strategy and organisational development can reshape the relationship between social characteristics and political competition.

Overall takeaway

Cleavages are not simply inherited from society: they are also politically constructed. Parties can reshape the political meaning of social differences through ideological repositioning, coalition-building and organisational expansion. Fidesz demonstrates how this process can consolidate a new political camp. Yet agency remains constrained by the social structures and institutional conditions within which political actors operate.
